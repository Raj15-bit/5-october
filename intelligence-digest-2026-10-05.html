The world's borrowing cost hit a 24-year high, and Indian shares paid the bill

Why a strong Indian economy and a falling Indian market sit side by side, and what ties them together.

In short
  • The cost of borrowing for the US government climbed to its highest level since 2002.
  • Foreign funds sold Indian shares day after day, so the Nifty fell for 8 weeks in a row.
  • Yet Indian factories, tax collections and bank loans all grew fast, so the problem is money, not the economy.

The single biggest force in the material is the price of money. A yield is what a lender demands each year to part with money for a fixed time. The US 10-year yield closed near 5.31%, a level last seen in 2002, and every other market is being priced against it.

India is the clearest case of a good economy caught in a bad money cycle. Factory orders, tax collections and bank lending all grew at double-digit speeds, but foreign investors sold shares for 7 sessions in a row and global funds kept a record bet against the Nifty. Why good numbers did not save the market

The posts kept returning to one puzzle: India's economy grew 7.8% in April to June and the largest listed companies grew profit 18%, yet the Sensex lost thousands of points since early August. 3 forces explain it, and none of them is Indian.

  • Dear US money — a US Treasury now pays about 5.3% with no currency risk, so a foreign fund needs a strong reason to hold Indian shares instead.
  • Dear oil — Brent stayed close to $100 a barrel, which widens India's import bill and pushes the rupee down.
  • The AI trade — global money chased chip and AI stocks in Korea, Taiwan and the US; India has few such companies.
  • Taxes and rules — some argued that India's taxes on share trading and gains push foreign money away; others said flows will follow rates, not taxes.

One view is that this is a classic bottom: pessimism is extreme, foreign funds are already very short, and Indian buyers keep absorbing the selling. The opposite view is that nothing changes until US yields fall, because a 5.3% safe return keeps pulling money out of every emerging market, India included.

The war has moved from oil to ships, pipes and refineries

Oil itself is flowing again. Crude exports from the Persian Gulf are back near 80% of pre-war levels as the US guards a shipping lane along Oman's coast. The shortage has moved down the chain: to tankers, to refineries and to diesel, which is why prices have not fallen as much as the flows suggest. Section 02 follows this in detail.

So the oil shock now works more like a tax on transport. Hiring a supertanker from the Gulf to Asia costs about 26 times what it did before the war, and freight is now more than a quarter of the cost of a barrel landed at a refinery. That cost reaches Indian buyers through diesel, jet fuel and plastics, not only through the crude price.

India's own engine is still running

Below the market noise, the Indian data in the material is strong. The September PMI (a monthly survey of purchasing managers; a reading above 50 means growth) rose to 55.1. Banks grew loans by close to 19% from a year earlier, and car makers, two-wheeler makers and lorry makers all sold more in September.

  • Factories — output and new orders grew fastest since February; export orders came from the US, Europe, the UAE and Brazil.
  • Tax — gross GST rose 14.7%; imports paid 25.9% more tax, partly because imported goods cost more.
  • Loans — 30 banks together grew loans 19.5% and deposits 16.1% from a year earlier.
  • Prices — SBI Research expects retail inflation to rise to 5.65% in September from 4.82% in August, which may stop the RBI from helping.
  • Rain — the monsoon ended about 15% below normal, and drought covers 265 of Maharashtra's 358 talukas.

So the Indian economy has 2 soft spots, prices and rain, and both are worth watching in the second half of the year. Neither explains the market fall on its own, but rising prices could make the RBI raise its rate, which would add a home-made reason to the foreign one.

Where the money went instead

Money did not leave India; it left the big companies in the index. Small-company and mid-company funds took the largest inflows in August, monthly SIPs (fixed monthly investments in mutual funds) hit a record, and foreign funds kept buying new share sales even while selling listed shares. Section 04 shows this split.

That is why some smaller companies tied to power, data centres and defence hit new highs on the same days the Nifty fell. The themes that are driving India's spending, such as transformers, cables, data centres and drug-making for foreign clients, barely sit inside the Nifty 50. So the index is a poor guide to where Indian profit growth is now coming from.

How the chain runs

The chain below links the main forces in the order the posts described them, from a war at sea to an Indian share price.

The chain also shows what would break it. A lasting deal over Hormuz, or a fall in US yields, would remove the first 2 links. Until then, Indian investors are paying a price set in Washington and in the Gulf, which means the best Indian news of the season may go unrewarded for some time.

02

Oil is flowing again, but tankers, diesel and refineries are now the shortage

Hormuz, the Red Sea, the Saudi pipeline and what all of it costs an Indian fuel buyer.

In short
  • Crude oil is moving out of the Gulf at close to 80% of its pre-war level.
  • But ships to carry it and refineries to turn it into diesel are short, so fuel stays dear.
  • Indian buyers are paying through petrol pump prices, airline fuel charges and freight.

3 claims about Hormuz that cannot all be true

The Strait of Hormuz is the channel between Iran and Oman that carries about a fifth of the world's oil. 3 sets of statements about it ran side by side, and they point in different directions.

The 3 fit together once "open" is split in 2. The strait is open for Saudi, Iraqi, Kuwaiti and Emirati oil moving in US-guarded convoys. It is shut for Iran, whose loadings fell to zero in August. So flows are near normal, while the risk of each trip is not, and that risk is what tanker owners and insurers price.

The ship is now dearer than ever

Freight costs rose far faster than oil. Hiring a very large crude carrier (VLCC, a tanker that holds about 2 million barrels) from the Gulf to Asia now costs about $1.3 million a day. Shipbroker Poten & Partners estimates freight is now more than a quarter of the cost of a barrel at a refinery gate.

  • Before the war — the same tanker cost less than $50,000 a day, and freight was about 3% of the cost of a landed barrel.

One view is that the true limit is now physical. A tanker cannot be printed, and every ship hit or held back removes capacity for months, so even a fully open strait would leave a shortage. That favours ship owners: Great Eastern Shipping has 3 Suezmax tankers (ships sized for the Suez Canal) on spot rates, which are up 3.5 times this year, while its shares trade about 30% below the value of its fleet.

Diesel and refineries: the second shortage

Diesel moves trucks, tractors and ships, so its price travels into almost every good. Refined products leaving the Gulf are only about half of pre-war levels, partly because Gulf refineries hit early in the war are still not fully repaired. Russia, which sold about 12% of the world's exported diesel, has extended a halt on its diesel exports to 31 October after Ukrainian drone strikes.

  • G7 release — the G7 countries agreed to release 100 million barrels of oil and diesel from emergency stocks over 4 months; Europe alone will release 50 million barrels of diesel over 2 months.
  • US diesel — wholesale diesel in New York is about 15% below its mid-September record, but still very dear in plain terms.
  • US refineries — no large full refinery has been built in the US since 1977; 2 California and Texas plants shut in 2025, so plants run at 95% to 98% of capacity.
  • Kuwait — pumping just over 2 million barrels a day against 2.6 million before the war; its oil company chief says crude is plentiful but refined fuel is short.
  • Goldman Sachs — warns the refining squeeze could last into 2027.

This is why one argument in the posts says investors in India should watch the diesel margin, the gap between diesel and crude prices, rather than crude alone. When diesel rises faster than crude, transport costs rise for every company that moves goods, even if the headline oil price looks calm.

The Saudi pipeline and the Red Sea

Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline carries crude across the country to the Red Sea, so oil can avoid Hormuz. It was attacked again at a pumping station east of Riyadh and kept flowing, while smoke was seen over a refinery in Riyadh and the Ras Tanura plant was also targeted. Saudi Aramco's chief Amin Nasser said that without this pipeline Brent would be near $200.

At the same time Yemen's government, backed by Saudi Arabia, began a full offensive to retake land held by the Houthis, an Iran-backed group that had blocked the Bab el-Mandeb strait at the southern end of the Red Sea. Government forces claimed gains near the strait, and the Saudi-led coalition said about 100 jets joined the attack. The US refused Saudi requests to join with air strikes.

  • If the offensive works — the Red Sea route to Europe could reopen, which would cut tanker voyage times and freight costs.
  • If it stalls — Houthi missiles on Saudi oil sites and on shipping near the strait would keep rising.

Sellers are cutting prices to Asia

The most surprising price signal came from sellers, not buyers. Saudi Arabia set its November price for Arab Light crude to Asia at $5 a barrel below the Dubai benchmark, its widest discount since the Covid crash, when traders had expected a premium. In the same period Indian Oil Corporation bought 2 million barrels of Iraq's Basrah Medium from the trader Vitol at a $32 discount to Dubai, a figure confirmed by several reports.

The discount is large because the buyer, not the seller, carries the risk of the trip through Hormuz. So cheap crude for Indian refiners comes with higher freight and insurance, which eats much of the saving. It still helps refiners such as Chennai Petroleum and Mangalore Refinery, which some brokers named as top picks.

Iran's economy is breaking

Iran is paying the highest price for the blockade. US Treasury secretary Scott Bessent said Iran faces zero oil revenue for the first time in its history, and its oil minister Mohsen Paknejad resigned. The open-market rial has fallen 78% against the dollar since June, as the line chart shows.

US officials met at Camp David to discuss Iran, and President Trump spoke of an "easy way or hard way". So a sharp move, either a deal or a strike, is more likely in the coming weeks, and either would move oil and freight sharply.

What Indian buyers pay

The shock reached Indian pumps and planes through private sellers first, because state-run pumps have held prices. Europe faces a thin winter

Europe's gas shortage matters to India because Asian and European buyers compete for the same gas ships.

  • Storage — about 71.5% full against a 15-year average near 88%.
  • Qatar — shipped only 4 cargoes of liquefied gas in September against about 30 in a normal month.
  • Coal — gas above €80 a megawatt-hour made coal cheaper than gas for power for the first time in years, so utilities burn more coal.

Any cold spell would make Asian and European buyers bid against each other for the same cargoes, which would raise gas costs for Indian fertiliser makers and city-gas firms too.

03

The price of money: why US bonds keep falling whatever the news

A rate rise, weak jobs, AI borrowing, France's debt and what it all means for the world's savers.

In short
  • The Fed raised its rate in September, its first rise since 2023.
  • Bond prices kept falling even on bad jobs news, because the supply of new bonds is huge.
  • A large part of that new borrowing now comes from the AI building boom.

Every piece of news sends yields up

When bond prices fall, their yields rise; they always move in opposite directions. The US Federal Reserve raised its benchmark rate on 16 September to a range of 3.75% to 4%, its first rise since 2023, confirmed by several reports. Then a weak September jobs report briefly pulled yields down, and within the hour they were climbing again.

US 10-year Treasury yield at key moments, %

Yields quoted in the material; the last point is 5 October

A chart in the posts summed up the mood: a rate rise, hawkish talk, Treasury buybacks, lower inflation and a bad jobs report all ended with higher yields. When a market ignores news that should help it, the cause is usually supply, not the news.

Goldman Sachs noted the long-term yield has risen about 133 basis points from its February low, almost exactly the rise of the 2013 "taper tantrum".

A basis point is 0.01 percentage point, so 133 basis points is 1.33 points.

  • Rate-rise odds — the chance of another Fed rise on 28 October fell from about 70% to about 24% after the jobs report.
  • 2-year yield — about 4.82%; the gap to the 10-year widened from 18 to 48 basis points in under 2 weeks.
  • 30-year yield — about 5.65% to 5.75%, near its highest in about 2 decades.
  • 5-year auction — sold at 5.033%, the first yield above 5% since 2007, with one of the weakest bids on record.
  • Mortgages — the 30-year US mortgage rate is about 7.60%.

The AI boom has become a bond issuer

The most argued-over claim was that AI companies are now part of why yields rise. Amazon, Alphabet, Microsoft and Meta sold close to $200 billion of bonds in 2026, more than in the previous 5 years combined.

On top of that, one tracker puts hyperscalers' off-balance-sheet commitments (leases, purchase promises and guarantees not shown as debt) at about $3.6 trillion, up about $500 billion in a single month.

India's Economic Affairs Secretary Anuradha Thakur made the same point in New Delhi: global bond yields can no longer be explained by central banks and deficits alone, and the scale of the AI build-out is part of the story. That is an unusual thing for a finance ministry to say aloud, and it means Indian policy makers see AI spending abroad as a cost to India through higher global rates.

One view is that this is a debt mountain hidden off the books, and that the risk of the chips falling in value is not priced. The opposite view is that the biggest borrowers earn hundreds of billions a year, so the debt is affordable and the demand for AI computing is real. Section 09 sets out the financing chain in detail.

Who still wants long bonds?

  • Foreign central banks — no longer buy US bonds without regard to price; Japan's long yields are the highest since 1996.
  • US debt — above $40 trillion and about 100% of GDP, the first time since just after the Second World War; the average maturity is only 2.6 years, so old cheap debt rolls into new dear debt fast.
  • Retail buyers — small US investors bought $170 million of a long-bond fund in 3 days, their biggest purchase in at least a year, even as that fund is down 55% from 2020.
  • Michael Hartnett of Bank of America — says it is time to "start adding some bonds", since long Treasuries have had their worst returns in 100 years.

Views split sharply. One side says bonds are now cheap and a slowing jobs market will bring yields down. The other side says the US runs a deficit near 7% of GDP while the economy is hot, so a recession would push it toward 12% and need even more bonds.

A third, more unusual view is that the biggest tech shares have replaced long bonds as the "safe" asset, which is why a fund of the largest US tech companies rose alongside the long-bond yield.

The breadth under the US market is weak. More large US stocks hit yearly lows than highs for 14 days running, and Morgan Stanley's Mike Wilson said half of US stocks have already had bear-market falls while the index sits near a record. Goldman Sachs found that hedge funds have cut their borrowing to the lowest since the April 2025 tariff shock.

France is Europe's weak spot

The gap between French and German long-term yields widened by 32 basis points to 141 in a single week, the largest weekly jump on record.

Markets now price French debt close to Italy's despite a better credit rating. The euro fell to its weakest level in 17 months, and Spain's prime minister called an early election for 29 November.

One view is that France is where the next euro debt crisis starts, because French banks are tied to their government's rating. The opposite view, from Deutsche Bank's Jim Reid, is that the fall itself acts as a brake: higher French yields make it harder for the European Central Bank to raise rates, and its expected peak rate already dropped from 3.5% to about 3.2%.

Brazil: the other emerging market

Flávio Bolsonaro, a right-wing senator, finished ahead of President Lula in Brazil's first round on 4 October, about 47% to 45%, sending the race to a runoff on 25 October. Brazil's real jumped about 4.5% against the dollar and its main share index rose sharply.

  • Valuation — Brazil trades near 10 times expected profit against 20 to 21 times for India, with faster expected profit growth.
  • Rates — Brazil's central bank has started cutting from 14.25% to 13.75% while inflation fell to about 4.2%, so real rates are very high and can fall.
  • Oil — Brazil exports oil, so a dear barrel helps it while it hurts India.

One view is that Brazil is now the better emerging-market bet than India on price alone. The opposite view is that Brazil's gains rest on 1 election and on oil, while India's profit growth is steadier; the October 25 runoff will test the first side quickly.

Gold and the dollar

Central banks keep buying gold to depend less on the dollar, and the posts read this as a slow shift rather than a collapse of the dollar.

  • Central banks — have bought about 1,000 tonnes of gold a year for 4 years.
  • Reserve managers — 74% expect the dollar's share of reserves, now about 57%, to fall over 5 years, in a World Gold Council survey.
  • Price — gold swung from about $5,500 an ounce in January to $4,000 in June and sits near $5,350, while gold funds keep shrinking.
04

India's market: 8 weeks of falls, record bets against it, and money that moved, not left

Foreign selling, Indian buying, the shrinking index giants and the case for and against a bottom.

In short
  • The Nifty fell for 8 straight weeks, its longest run of falls in 25 years.
  • Foreign funds sold heavily while Indian funds bought almost all of it.
  • Money is moving from the big index names to smaller companies and new listings.

A losing streak not seen since 2001

The Nifty and Sensex both fell for 8 weeks in a row to early October, the longest such run in 25 years, confirmed by several market reports. The Nifty closed at 22,555.75 on 5 October after a weak start.

  • 200-day average — the Nifty has spent about 148 straight sessions below its 200-day moving average (the average closing price of the past 200 days), the longest stretch since 2016.
  • Calendar year — on course for its first yearly fall since 2015; down about 13.4% in 2026, with September alone -6.1%.
  • 12-month return — about -9%, the worst since the Covid crash.
  • Car makers — the auto index fell for 8 weeks, its longest streak since Covid.
  • Seasons — October is the only month with a negative average Sensex return over the past 46 years.

Borrowed money in shares is at a record

SIPs: record money, short memories

Monthly SIP flows have tripled in 5 years, and there are about 10.75 crore SIP accounts. But about half of all SIP money has been invested for less than 2 years.

  • Staying power — only 27% of SIP money has stayed more than 4 years.
  • Churn — 7.19 crore new SIPs started last year and 6.80 crore stopped or ended.

So the domestic buyer that has been absorbing foreign selling is young and untested by a long fall. If a falling market makes many of these investors stop their SIPs, the main support under Indian shares would weaken just when it is needed.

Is the bottom near?

One view is contrarian. The broad market's 2-year return has turned negative, a rare event; in the past, the years that followed returned a middle value of about 31% a year.

The Nifty's 21,700 to 22,200 zone has held twice, and strong September-quarter results after a strong June quarter could turn sentiment.

The opposite view is that flows, not value, set prices now. As long as a US Treasury pays 5.3%, foreign funds will return to India in size only when global rates fall, as Sankaran Naren put it. RBI Governor Sanjay Malhotra added a twist: a correction in AI shares abroad could send money back to India. So the trigger may be a fall elsewhere, not good news at home.

The RBI's policy decision on 7 October is the near-term test. Views split: some brokers expect a 25 basis point rise because of rising prices and a weak rupee, others expect a hold because the RBI already drained cash through bond sales (section 05). A hold with a calm tone would help banks and rate-sensitive shares; a rise would hit them first.

Banks: record growth on paper, thanks to $133 billion of NRI deposits

What the September-quarter bank updates really show once the special dollar deposits are taken out.

In short
  • Banks reported their fastest growth in years, but much of it came from a one-time dollar deposit scheme.
  • Loans are still growing faster than deposits, and cheap savings deposits are shrinking as a share.
  • Views split on whether state-run or private banks will win the next leg.

The dollar window that lifted every bank

To support the rupee, the RBI ran a special window until 31 August that let banks raise FCNR(B) deposits (foreign currency deposits from Indians living abroad) and swap the dollars with the RBI on easy terms. Banks raised about $133 billion through it, a huge sum against India's bank system. That is why many banks reported their best growth in 7 to 10 years.

  • HDFC Bank — raised $11.5bn of these deposits; deposits grew 18.8%, the fastest in 9 quarters.
  • Axis Bank — raised $10.62bn; deposit growth of 20.7% was the best in 28 quarters, but 17.0% without the scheme.
  • Bank of Baroda — about $8bn; Emkay says 75% to 80% is already lent out.
  • Kotak Mahindra Bank — $5.78bn; net loans up 24.7%.
  • IndusInd Bank — $3.51bn; without it, deposits would have shrunk 4.7% from June.
  • IDFC First Bank — about $3.6bn; it slowed costly bulk deposits because it had more cash than it needed.

Much of this money came back as loans. Banks lent dollars to the same NRI depositors at their GIFT City and overseas branches, sometimes up to 9 times the deposit, so investors could earn the gap between rates.

Loans against fixed deposits jumped 43.2% in August to ₹2.04 lakh crore, the fastest rise in over 3 years. These loans pad the headline growth but earn banks little.

Across 30 banks that reported, loans grew 19.5% and deposits 16.1% from a year earlier, so loans now equal a bigger share of deposits. RBI data for the whole system show the same gap. The chart compares loan and deposit growth bank by bank.

The RBI sold bonds to soak up the cash

The dollar scheme had a side effect. When banks handed dollars to the RBI, the RBI gave them rupees, and surplus cash in the banking system swelled to about ₹10 lakh crore to ₹11 lakh crore. Overnight rates fell below the RBI's policy rate, which weakened its control.

So the RBI sold ₹1 lakh crore of government bonds in 3 auctions in September, its first big net sale in about 2 years.

Selling a bond takes cash out of the banks, because they pay for the bonds with spare cash. It is not a rate rise, but it tightens money in a similar way, and India's 10-year yield rose 43 basis points to 7.18% in the quarter.

State-run or private banks?

One view, from Anand Rathi's banking report titled "As Good as It Gets", is that state-run banks keep winning. They have outgrown private banks for 8 straight quarters, lend only about 81% of their deposits against about 91% at private banks, and have the lowest new bad loans.

Its top picks include SBI, ICICI Bank, Axis Bank, Indian Bank, Bank of Baroda, Union Bank, Karur Vysya Bank, City Union Bank, DCB Bank and J&K Bank.

The opposite view, from Goldman Sachs, is that state-run banks are now losing share. Their home loan and deposit growth trails the system, the extra cash they held has shrunk, and the dollar scheme gave private banks easy funding, so private banks are at a turning point. Emkay sided with selected names: Karur Vysya Bank was its "cleanest" update, while Bandhan Bank was "the weak link" and AU Small Finance Bank looked dear at 2.5 times book value.

  • Valuation — banks trade at about 1.6 times book value and 12.2 times profit, about 5% below their 5-year and 10-year averages; private banks about 18% below their 10-year average.
  • Margins — net interest margin (the gap between what a bank earns on loans and pays on deposits) steady near 3.2%; new deposit rates have fallen more than old ones, so costs can ease.
  • Risk 1, jobs — hiring at the top 100 companies stalled, with headcount flat last year; the top 20 IT firms have not added staff since 2023, a risk to salaried borrowers.
  • Risk 2, rain — the monsoon was about 15% below normal; reservoirs are 21% lower than a year ago, putting farm, microfinance and small-business loans at risk later this year.

New chiefs at HDFC Bank and Kotak

The RBI approved Anup Bagchi, formerly of ICICI Bank, as HDFC Bank's chief executive from 27 October, and Kotak Mahindra Bank named Anup Saha, also an ICICI veteran, as its next chief. HDFC Bank's US-listed shares jumped about 6% on the news.

  • Brokers' targets — Citi ₹970, JPMorgan ₹990, Morgan Stanley ₹1,025 and Kotak Institutional Equities ₹1,050.
  • This year — the stock has fallen about 32% and trailed the bank index by 19 points.

One view is that an outside chief ends the uncertainty that hung over HDFC Bank and will bring a sharper performance culture. The opposite view is that the market cheered too fast: in 2020 the stock rallied on Sashidhar Jagdishan's approval and then lost about 40%; Mr Bagchi has run an insurer and a brokerage but never a bank, and Mr Saha spent about 9 months each at Bajaj Finance and Kotak. Whether either can steer a bank through a bad credit cycle is still untested.

  • Bajaj Finance — assets under management up 26.5% to ₹5.85 lakh crore, the best in 7 quarters, and 128.85 million customers; it plans to raise up to ₹17,500 crore through a QIP and warrants to its parent, about 17% of its core capital, which puzzled analysts.
  • Bajaj Housing Finance — loans disbursed ₹19,930 crore, up 25%; assets ₹1.58 lakh crore.
  • Poonawalla Fincorp — assets up 55% to ₹73,950 crore.
  • Sammaan Capital — disbursals of about ₹6,000 crore against ₹3,875 crore in the June quarter; 30,700 new customers.
  • L&T Finance — retail disbursals ₹24,000 crore, up 27%; gold loans more than 3 times a year ago.
  • Mahindra & Mahindra Financial Services — disbursals up 22%; spare cash 11% of assets; Emkay calls it a clean recovery story.
  • OnEMI Technology Solutions (Kissht) — assets up 68.4% to ₹9,317 crore; 79.5 million registered users.
  • SG Finserve — loan book about ₹5,694 crore, up about 98%.

Microfinance: clean books, dry fields

Microfinance lenders, which make small loans to poor households, have cleaned up after a bad stretch. CreditAccess Grameen cut loans overdue 90 days or more to 1.1%, and Muthoot Microfin raised its yearly growth target to 20%.

But drought is the new risk. Maharashtra has declared drought in about 74% of its talukas and Karnataka in 177, and the 2 states hold more than a sixth of India's microfinance loans. Floods in Bihar, Uttar Pradesh, Chhattisgarh and Madhya Pradesh in August and September already nudged up new overdue loans. So the clean-up could be tested again in the second half, which is why lenders are slowing in those regions.

India's economy and policy: tax relief, factory jobs moving east, and a trade deal that stalled

The GST overhaul, jobs, prices, rain, trade talks and the state reforms that came up.

In short
  • The GST Council meets to give businesses back tax paid on machines and to ease rules for small sellers.
  • Factory jobs are growing fastest in the east and south, but wages are rising faster than output per worker.
  • Trade talks with the US have stalled, while India signs deals elsewhere.

GST 2.0: money back for factories, less fear for small firms

The biggest is a refund, in monthly parts over 5 years, of GST paid on plant and machinery, which works like an interest-free loan for every company building a factory.

Average pay in factories rose to about ₹3.8 lakh a year, while output per worker grew only 2%. So wages are rising faster than productivity, which squeezes factory margins unless companies invest in machines. Food, electrical equipment, metal goods and medicines added the most jobs.

The quality of jobs is a separate worry. Prices and rain: the 2 soft spots

  • Inflation — SBI Research expects retail inflation of 5.65% in September, up from 4.82%, and above 6.5% in October and November.
  • Monsoon — about 15% below normal; the south and the east and north-east each about 26% short; 17 of the weather regions, covering about 47% of the land, had too little rain.
  • Drought — declared in 265 of Maharashtra's 358 talukas and 177 in Karnataka; farmers in Akola showed dried crops and rising debts.
  • Edible oil — the government cut import duty on soybean, sunflower and palm oil on 23 September to hold down cooking-oil prices.
  • Sugar — the government will stop letting mills turn cane juice and B-heavy molasses into ethanol, after a sugar price shock; cane is now only about 9% of ethanol supply.

So the RBI faces a hard choice: prices are rising and the rupee is weak, which argue for a higher rate, while growth in farm incomes is at risk, which argues against. Higher food prices in the coming months would also hit the shoppers that consumer companies depend on (section 07).

A small fact that was widely misread

Reports said UPI payments "fell" in September after a proposal to charge fees on them. But September has 1 day fewer than August, about 3% fewer days, while UPI volume fell only 1.8%. Per day, UPI use actually rose, so the fee fear did not show up in the numbers.

Trade: stalled with the US, moving with others

Finance Minister Nirmala Sitharaman said trade talks with the US have reached a "plateau" where further giving and taking will be "very, very difficult", and the latest round ended on 5 October without a deal. Some read a clear change of tone in New Delhi toward Washington. External Affairs Minister S Jaishankar said the US "has deliberately become a lonelier power".

  • Canada — the 5th round of talks on a trade pact (CEPA) runs 5 to 9 October in Ottawa, with a goal to finish by December; trade was $30.4 billion in 2025.
  • Switzerland and EFTA — Swiss President Guy Parmelin visited on the 1st anniversary of the EFTA pact, which ties tariff cuts to a promised $100 billion of investment and 1 million jobs over 15 years.
  • Russia — expected to employ about 70,000 skilled Indian workers this year, mainly in building and logistics; Russia expects a shortage of 3.1 million workers by 2030.
  • G20 — at the trade ministers' meeting in Milwaukee, Piyush Goyal rejected Western efforts to brand building factories as "overcapacity"; no agreement was reached.
  • China — Ms Sitharaman said India's trade deficit with China has grown sharply since 2014, a sign the global trading system has become lopsided.

One view is that India should give up on a US deal and diversify to Europe, Canada and the Gulf. The other view is that the US is still India's largest export market, and that a lasting tariff gap will push factory investment to rivals such as Vietnam. The Canada round, ending 9 October, is the next sign of whether the diversification is real.

States are changing old rules

  • West Bengal — Finance Minister Swapan Dasgupta said the state will scrap its 1976 urban land ceiling law, which caps a single land purchase at 5,000 square metres; Chief Minister Suvendu Adhikari also promised a uniform civil code within 6 months.
  • West Bengal industry — a new industrial policy will focus on AI, chips and data centres, and pay more than ₹10,000 crore of overdue incentives, with small firms first.
  • West Bengal airports — 4 new airports under the UDAN scheme in 2 years, and ₹29,000 crore for Bengal's aviation over a decade.
  • Delhi EV rules — 382 charging sites approved; all new 3-wheelers and small goods vehicles must be electric from January 2027, and new petrol two-wheelers cannot be registered from April 2028.
  • Gas pipes — a national drive aims for 50 lakh new piped-gas home connections in the year; gas is only about 6% of India's energy use.

India registered 330,000 electric vehicles in September, and electric cars reached about 36,000, nearly double a year earlier. So the fuel shock is speeding up the switch to electric, as the oil shocks of the 1970s slowed oil demand in rich countries.

Mumbai's airport squeeze

Mumbai airport, run by Adani Airports, plans to cut about 265 weekly international departure slots, about a third of those at its main international terminal, from 25 October, so it can rebuild its 60-year-old domestic terminal from 2027. Airlines and the global airline body IATA object, saying the winter schedule was approved in June. Passengers would face longer trips to the new Navi Mumbai airport, fewer flights or higher fares.

Financial boom, slow real economy?

A study by Azim Premji University, discussed in the posts, argues that India ranks 14th in the world by stock market value against GDP but 146th by output per person.

Household debt rose from about a third of GDP to 41% over 5 years, and the study says borrowing is replacing wage growth while company investment stays weak.

One view is that this shows a financial boom running ahead of the real economy. The other view, supported by the factory data above, is that the real economy is now catching up through jobs in the east and south. A related problem is that India funds deep-tech startups but rarely buys from them: only 58 defence startup prototypes were cleared, with 45 orders worth ₹2,326 crore, so many young firms die waiting for a first customer.

The labs' arithmetic

AI labs such as OpenAI and Anthropic use only about 30% to 40% of their computing for selling answers; the rest trains new models. That share decides how much money each gigawatt makes.

  • Example — a lab with 5 GW that uses 2 GW for paid answers and earns $100 billion a year makes $50 billion per paid gigawatt, but only $20 billion across all its computing.

So as labs grow from 5 GW to 10 GW, their revenue must roughly double just to keep the same return, unless they spend less on training.

Anthropic's share-sale filing said it expects to spend about $518 billion on AI computing over 10 years. One view is that lab valuations are absurd: Meta valued at Anthropic's revenue multiple would be worth about $12.5 trillion. The opposite view is that comparing a lab growing faster than almost any company in history with a mature giant misses the growth.
